Moving from a spreadsheet to an app does not automatically fix a disorganized calendar. If the sheet mixes confirmed appointments, requests and cancellations without a clear rule, moving everything preserves the confusion. **Migration starts by defining what each record means.**

This guide proposes an adoption process you can also use when evaluating another system. It does not assume AntraPro offers automatic Excel imports or synchronization with external tools.

## Identify where the real calendar lives

There may be a spreadsheet, a notebook and messages on two phones. Ask which record the team checks before serving someone and who may change it. If the answer depends on the person, agree on a reference source before changing tools.

Write down specific problems: duplicate appointments, incomplete information, services without a duration or availability only the owner knows. The goal should be verifiable, such as “everyone checks tomorrow's appointments in the same place,” rather than simply “digitize the business.”

## Clean up what you need to start

You do not have to reconstruct years of conversations. Start with active services, the people providing them and upcoming confirmed appointments. Review the information with someone who knows the operation.

| Information | What to check before moving it |
| --- | --- |
| Services | Clear names, duration and current price where applicable |
| Team | Who provides each service and their working hours |
| Clients | Duplicates and necessary contact information |
| Upcoming appointments | Date, time, professional and actual status |
| Notes | Useful context; avoid copying entire conversations |

Do not merge two clients just because they share a name. Do not move personal notes that are unnecessary for providing the service. Keeping less, carefully selected information also makes it easier to maintain.

## Choose a transition date

Define the day from which new appointments are created in the chosen system and what happens to earlier appointments. Keep the old spreadsheet as a reference when needed, but make clear which system governs daily operations.

Two editable calendars without coordination can create more conflicts than one spreadsheet. If a transition period is necessary, assign someone to reconcile them and set an end date. Do not leave it as an indefinite task for “everyone.”

Before publishing a booking link, record upcoming appointments or block occupied times using the available features. A public page cannot know about commitments you never entered.

## Test with a small working day

Check business hours, each professional's availability and service duration. Then walk through a test booking with authorized information and make sure you know how to identify and remove it without confusing it with a real appointment.

Check what the client receives and what the team sees. An email confirmation may be part of the flow, but review addresses, service details and the time. Do not promise automatic reminders merely because you saw a confirmation.

## Train the team on exceptions

Creating an appointment is usually easy to demonstrate. Spend time on what happens afterward: a client asks to change the time, a staff member is absent or a service takes longer than planned. Decide who can resolve each situation and where the change is recorded.

Provide a short instruction: where to check the calendar, where to record changes and whom to notify if something does not match. You do not need a fifty-page manual to agree on these rules.

## Measure whether the change helps

During the first weeks, record how many appointments required corrections, how many availability questions the team answered manually and which questions recur. Where possible, compare with an earlier period with a similar volume.

Those figures do not, by themselves, prove a tool increased sales. They do help identify configuration mistakes and determine whether the process is clearer. If problems remain, distinguish information quality, training and features the product does not cover.
