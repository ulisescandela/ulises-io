Every week someone claims to have built a million-dollar business "solo, with AI." They almost never tell you how many contractors they had, which channel brought in customers, or how many months they sat below $1,000 before the month they posted on social media.

I wanted to put in order what is actually known. This article is a map by monthly revenue level (from $10k to $100M USD): what the business usually looks like at each one, how it gets the sale, what it does with AI and, above all, **what the level below is missing that the level above solves**.

A warning up front: this is a model, not a statistic. I mark when something is published data and when it is my own estimate. The ranges for customers, costs and hours are reasoned orders of magnitude, not measurements. Use them to locate yourself, not to plan to the dollar.

## What the data says before the model

The most solid data I found comes from Stripe (companies created with Stripe Atlas) and from MicroConf's annual survey of independent SaaS. Both are biased: Atlas over-represents US startups, and MicroConf is a self-selected survey. Even so, they draw a clear pattern.

* **Solo founding is at an all-time high.** According to Stripe, founders without a co-founder were 63% of the C corps formed in the second quarter of 2026, a record.
* **Results are very uneven.** Stripe reports that the top 10% of solo founders from 2025 earned about 61 times the median in their first six months; four years ago that gap was about 34 times. Median revenue for that period fell 23% compared with the previous year, while the top decile's rose 19%.
* **The winners share five traits:** AI-native products, selling internationally from the start (51% of their revenue comes from outside their home market versus 2% for the median), a B2B focus (at 24 months the median solo B2B business earns more than four times what the median B2C one does), better first-month retention (around 30% versus 8%) and recurring billing.
* **Solo doesn't mean better.** Stripe also found that, at 24 months, the top decile of multi-founder startups generates 53% more revenue than the top decile of solo founders. The gap nearly disappears at the 99th percentile of bootstrapped companies.
* **Most people are far from the headlines.** In MicroConf's 2024 survey, the largest group (28%) made less than $1,000 in MRR, and 44% operated with teams of two to five people.

Two consequences for reading the rest: AI made building cheaper, so what is scarce now is distribution; and "solo" is usually a phase, not a destination.

## The famous case and what rarely gets told

The most-cited example of "one founder, 80 million dollars" is Base44, sold to Wix in June 2025. It is real and it is impressive. But TechCrunch confirmed with Wix that the company had eight employees when it was sold, and $25 million of the price was a retention bonus for them. The $80 million upfront price also came with milestone payments through 2029, and the founder had already co-founded and grown another well-funded data company. None of that takes away from the achievement; it does mean it isn't a recipe someone starting from zero can replicate.

That is why in the rest of the article I separate "solo + AI" from "solo + contractors" and from "small team."

## Map by level

The table summarizes the model. Every range is **my own estimate** unless stated otherwise. "Revenue" means gross recurring monthly revenue (or a six-month average), not your best month.

| $10k | Micro-SaaS, productized service, community or course | SaaS: 100–300 customers at $30–100 | Direct outreach, community, own audience | Common among those who already sell; rare in absolute terms |
| $50k | Niche B2B SaaS, premium service, cohorts | 250–1,000 customers at $50–200 | One compounding channel (SEO, integrations, content) | Possible but rare; already with 1–3 contractors |

Now, level by level and with the jumps between them.

## Level 1: $10k a month

**What it is.** A $49 micro-SaaS with 200 customers, a $2,500 productized service with four clients, or a course or community with a few dozen sales a month. Software margins are high (80–90% in my estimate); services drop to 50–70% because you do the work yourself.

**How it uses AI.** For 50–80% of the repetitive code, to summarize competitor reviews and spot recurring complaints, for content drafts and to personalize the first message to a prospect. What it doesn't delegate: customer interviews, demos and product decisions.

**How it sells.** The first ten sales are manual: your network, direct messages, niche communities, live demos. You close because the problem hurts and because you answer within hours. To go from ten to hundreds of customers you need a second channel that doesn't depend on you writing every message.

**Costs and hours (estimate).** Between $150 and $600 a month in tools and AI, almost nothing on advertising and 40–55 hours a week full time.

**Main risk.** Depending on a single channel or a single large customer, and support eating your day.

### The jump from $10k to $50k

* **What the lower level is missing:** a channel that scales without you, and a price anchored to value, not to the cost of the tool.
* **The lever:** raise the ticket two or three times toward the segment that pays the most, and swap the manual channel for one that compounds (SEO, integrations, partnerships, content). MicroConf found that products with an entry plan of $1,000 or more a month added around $1,073 in new monthly MRR, versus about $316 for those charging between $1 and $9.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** building whatever each customer asks for instead of going deep on the segment that pays more and cancels less. And lowering the price "to grow."
* **What the founder stops doing:** answering every ticket and running every demo. Document, automate onboarding and leave first-line support to a bot or an assistant.

## Level 2: $50k a month

**What it is.** A niche B2B SaaS with 250–500 customers at $99–199, or a premium service with ten clients at $5,000 (in practice a micro-agency, not "solo"). This is the practical ceiling of what one person with AI truly does. Estimated margin: 65–85% in software.

**How it uses AI.** Code agents with tests and CI (you move from writing to reviewing), churn analysis by cohort, SEO content with human review (mass SEO without review tends to lose traffic afterward), semi-automatic sales sequences and a bot that resolves a meaningful share of support. My estimate is between 40% and 70% of tickets.

**How it sells.** At least one compounding channel already works. Most sign-ups should be self-serve (my rule: 70% or more without human contact).

**Costs and hours (estimate).** $1–4k a month in tools, $2–8k in contractors, from $0 to $10k in advertising and 45–60 hours a week.

**Main risk.** Dependence on a platform (Google, a marketplace, the model API's pricing) and the risk that everything depends on you: if you're out for three weeks, the business degrades.

### The jump from $50k to $100k

* **What the lower level is missing:** retention and expansion. With 500 customers and 4% monthly churn you lose 20 customers every month before you grow by one.
* **The lever:** move from "more customers" to "more revenue per customer": usage-based or per-location plans, add-ons, annual billing paid upfront and a larger segment. A meaningful share of churn (between a fifth and a third, my estimate) tends to be involuntary, from declined cards, and it is fixed with payment retries.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** measuring only new sign-ups and ignoring net retention and failed payments.
* **What the founder stops doing:** coding everything. Hire or contract development or QA part time and spend half your time on distribution.

## Level 3: $100k a month

**What it is.** Around $1.2M in annual revenue. For example, a vertical SaaS with 500 accounts at $200, or a consumer AI app with about 7,000 subscribers at $15. Public cases at this level almost always have between two and six people around them. Margin moves between 55% and 75%; in AI apps it drops because of inference costs.

**How it uses AI.** Everything above plus agents for QA, bug triage, ad creative at scale and AI-first support. You decide pricing, partnerships, hiring and strategy.

**How it sells.** Two channels working, one usually paid with payback in under six to twelve months (my estimate). MicroConf reports that 29% of those running ads wait more than seven months to recover the investment or don't know whether it works: measuring this is not optional.

**Costs and hours (estimate).** $3–10k in tools and AI, $8–25k in contractors, $5–30k in ads.

**Main risk.** Concentration in a paid channel, price changes from the model provider, key contractors without documentation, and compliance (privacy, invoicing, tax structure).

### The jump from $100k to $200k

* **What the lower level is missing:** a predictable acquisition system, with acquisition cost, payback and customer value measured, and a second growth engine.
* **The lever:** make the product the channel (invitations, shareable templates, integrations), an affiliate or partner program, or assisted sales for large accounts.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** refusing to hire out of a "I'm solo" ideology.
* **What the founder stops doing:** being the bottleneck for every decision. Write processes and give real ownership of support and content to someone else.

## Level 4: $200k a month

**What it is.** Around $2.4M a year: an AI app with viral growth or a SaaS with the product as the channel. Estimated margin of 45–70%. Things AI doesn't cover well appear here: negotiating with partners, security reviews for large customers, hiring, managing people.

**How it sells.** Mass self-serve plus a small sales or customer success team for accounts of $500 or more a month. The bottleneck is no longer the product; it's the founder's management capacity and reliability (uptime, fraud, abuse).

**Team and costs (estimate).** Three to eight people, between $20k and $80k a month in payroll, and $10–60k in advertising.

**Main risk.** Fraud and chargebacks, dependence on one provider's model and talent retention.

### The jump from $200k to $500k

* **What the lower level is missing:** an organization. Each function needs an owner who isn't you.
* **The lever:** hire function leaders, raise capital or join a platform with distribution (the Base44 path with Wix, which SaaStr described as a distribution arbitrage).
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** measuring success by how few people you have instead of by revenue per employee and speed.
* **What the founder stops doing:** individual production work. Now they recruit, allocate capital and set strategy.

## Level 5: $500k a month

**What it is.** About $6M a year with a team of roughly 5 to 20 people. "Tiny team" listings show companies with $5–10M in annual revenue and 2–15 people, but those are self-reports compiled by third parties. My healthy range for small AI-native companies is $400k to $1M in annual revenue per employee.

**What changes.** AI multiplies a team; it doesn't replace it. Formal security (certifications such as SOC 2), finance and legal appear. Estimated margin of 35–65%. The new risk: a model lab shipping your feature as part of its own product.

### The jump from $500k to $1M

* **What the lower level is missing:** a dominant growth engine (brand, distribution or proprietary data).
* **The lever:** international and multi-segment expansion. It is consistent with what Stripe measured in its top decile: 51% of revenue comes from outside the home market.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** optimizing margin and control when the job is capturing the market while the window is open.
* **What the founder stops doing:** being the best engineer or marketer in the company. Their job becomes hiring people better than they are.

## Level 6: $1M a month

**What it is.** $12M a year. There are public claims of solo founders with more than $10M annualized, but they are self-reports, often "peak month times twelve," from people who also have an interest in promoting the story. When they exist, they tend to be consumer AI apps with virality on social media, contractors and margins squeezed by inference. Realistic team: 8 to 30 people.

### The jump from $1M to $10M

* **What the lower level is missing:** the capacity to execute in parallel across several markets, channels and products. It's an organizational jump, not a tooling one.
* **The lever:** capital (investment or financing against recurring revenue), enterprise sales or joining a large platform.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** a founder who still wants to operate as if they were solo puts a ceiling on the company.
* **What the founder stops doing:** operating. They become a real CEO.

## Levels 7 and 8: $10M and $100M a month

With one person they are not realistic, and neither are they for teams of dozens. At $10M a month ($120M a year) we're talking about 30 to 300 people, regulatory compliance, enterprise sales and negotiating compute capacity. As a reference, Calcalist reported that Base44, already inside Wix, reached $100 million in annual recurring revenue in nine months; to do that it had to grow far beyond the eight employees it had when it was sold. At $100M a month ($1.2B a year), the question is no longer "solo + AI" but how many agents per employee, and the concept of a one-person company stops applying.

### The jump from $10M to $100M

* **What the lower level is missing:** category scale: platform, ecosystem, global enterprise presence.
* **The lever:** acquisitions, multiple products, global sales and large capital.
* **The mistake that keeps you stuck:** believing efficiency substitutes for organizational scale.
* **What the founder stops doing:** deciding alone. They need a board, financial leadership and division leaders.

## Five patterns that repeat at every level

1. **Distribution beats product.** AI made building cheaper; what's scarce is attention. MicroConf identifies SEO and word of mouth as the highest-impact channels, and Stripe's top decile sells in many countries from the first month.
2. **Segment and price set the ceiling.** B2B outperforms B2C, and higher entry plans grow faster than cheap ones.
3. **Early retention predicts almost everything.** A product people abandon in the first month isn't fixed with more acquisition.
4. **"Solo" is a phase.** AI delays the first hire; it doesn't eliminate it. Small teams still outperform solo founders at the top.
5. **Survivorship bias dominates the conversation.** You see the 1% who post, and many of the people telling the story are selling a course or a community about "how I did it."

## How to know what level you're really at

Measure this, not your best month:

* **Average recurring revenue over the last six months**, excluding launches and one-off projects.
* **What percentage arrives without you stepping in.** If it's under 30% (my threshold), you still have a freelance job, not a business.
* **Monthly churn.** In B2B for small businesses, above 5% monthly is an alarm; below 3% is healthy (an estimate based on commonly cited ranges).
* **Concentration.** If one channel or one customer contributes more than half, you're one level lower in terms of risk.
* **The two-week test:** if you disappear for 14 days, does revenue drop, hold or rise?

And a quick bottleneck guide:

| Symptom | Bottleneck | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fewer than 10 paying customers after 3+ months | Positioning / ideal customer | 30 interviews; change segment or message |
| Sign-ups fine, but churn above 6% monthly | Product / activation | Guided onboarding, activation metric, annual billing |
| Churn fine, but sign-ups flat | Distribution | One channel, 90 days, measured by cost and payback |
| All fine, but you're saturated | Systems | Document, delegate support, automate |
| All fine, but revenue grows under 3% a month | Price and segment | Raise price, higher plan, upsells |

## My conclusion

With one person and AI, the realistic outcome is tens of thousands of dollars a month if you already have distribution. A hundred thousand is rare and almost always includes contractors; beyond that you're building a company, and that's fine. What really changes between one level and the next is almost never a new feature: it's a channel that compounds, a better price, retention that stops leaking and, sooner or later, the decision to stop doing everything yourself.

If you're starting out, the practical advice is boring: pick one ideal customer, sell manually until you have twenty or thirty paying customers, measure how many stay and only then look for a channel that repeats. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can one person with AI reach $100k a month?

It's possible but rare, and the known cases almost always include contractors. If you count only the people working full time, it can be one person; if you count everyone who participates, it rarely is.

### What kind of business is most likely to work solo?

According to Stripe's data, those with the most revenue at two years tend to be B2B, with recurring billing, AI-native and selling outside their own country from the start. These are trends among companies created on that platform, not a guarantee.

### Are the numbers from "solo founders with millions" reliable?

Treat them with caution. Many are self-reported, annualize a good month and come from people with an interest in selling the story. Look for audited figures or sources that confirm the real team size.
