For decades, knowing how to write code was in itself an advantage. If you were able to translate an idea into instructions that a machine would understand, you had something that most people did not. That was the barrier to entry, and also the moat that protected your salary.

In 2026 that barrier has collapsed. With a good prompt, anyone can generate a React component, a Python script or a SQL query in seconds. The uncomfortable question (and the most important for your career) is no longer "I know how to program?", but "what makes me valuable when writing code is no longer the bottleneck?". This article is my honest answer, and it is not going to be afraid: it is going to move your energy to where the real value is now.

## Value shifts toward scarce skills

There is an economic principle that should be tattooed: value is not in what is difficult, but in what is scarce. For years programming was both things at once, so we confuse "difficult" with "valuable". They were synonymous.

AI has broken that link. Generating code went from difficult to cheap, almost instantaneous. And when something becomes abundant, its price sinks. But this abundance does not destroy value: it displaces it. It appears right next to you, in tasks that AI does not know how to do for you.

It is exactly the pattern that has been repeated throughout the [history of programming](/en/history-of-programming-languages-and-technologies): the assembler made the binary cheap, high-level languages made the assembler cheap, and in each jump valuable work went up a level of abstraction. Generative AI is the next step, not the end of the ladder.

## What loses value (and why it hurts to recognize it)

Let's be clear about what is getting cheaper, because denying it is the fastest way to stay behind:

*   Write repetitive code (boilerplate): CRUDs, forms, configurations. AI produces them better and faster than you typing.
*   Remember exact syntax: it is no longer worth memorizing the order of the arguments of a function. It is consulted or self-completed.
*   Translate an already defined problem into code: if someone gives you the complete and clear specification, that last step is the most automatable of all.

If your professional identity is based only on these three things, it is normal to feel dizzy. The good news: they are the least interesting part of the work, and releasing them frees you for what matters.

## What becomes scarce (and therefore valuable)

Here is the other side of the balance. These are capabilities whose price rises precisely because AI does not cover them.

### 1. Define the problem well
AI responds to what you ask, not to what you need. Knowing how to formulate the right question (what to build, for whom, what is a "fact" and what is optional) is today more valuable than knowing how to type the solution. A bad prompt on a poorly understood problem produces perfect code for the wrong target.

### 2. Criteria and technical taste (taste)
AI gives you twenty plausible solutions. Choosing which of them is the good one (the simple, the maintainable one, the one that does not exploit you in production at three months) requires criteria, and the criterion is built with experience and with [solid foundations](/en/programming-logic-and-data-structures-guide). That smell to distinguish the elegant from the fragile is not discharged from a model.

### 3. Systems thinking
A model generates functions; it rarely understands your entire architecture, your business constraints or how a change here breaks something three services beyond. Seeing the whole system and its consequences remains deeply human. If you want to reinforce this, the [fundamentals of software architecture](/en/software-architecture-fundamentals-and-books) are an investment that does not depreciate.

### 4. Responsibility and trust
When something fails at 3 am, someone has to respond. AI does not sign the incident or make the decision. Being the person the team trusts to make difficult decisions and sustain them is a value that no model takes from you.

### 5. Distribution: that your work reaches someone
Knowing how to build was never enough; you have to know that what was built matters and arrives. Communicate, sell an idea, understand the user. In fact, this connects with something I wrote a long time ago: [ideas are worthless without execution](/en/ideas-need-execution), and in 2026 the execution includes knowing how to distribute.

## Table: where the value moves in 2026

|Before it was valuable because it was difficult|Now it is valuable because it is scarce|
|---|---|
|Write code without syntax errors|Deciding what code deserves to exist|
|Remember APIs and memory frameworks|Choosing the right solution among many|
|Implement an already defined spec|Define spec from chaos|
|Produce a lot of code fast|Produce the correct and maintainable system|
|Know how to program|Know how to think, communicate and hold yourself accountable|

## How to adapt without panic

There is no need to reinvent yourself from scratch. It is necessary to raise the level of abstraction, as the programmers did in each previous era:

1.  It uses AI as a lever, not as a crutch. Write the boilerplate so that you stay with the difficult-and-scarce: design, decisions, critical review.
2.  It cultivates judgment, not speed. Check code of people better than you. Always ask yourself why one solution is better than another.
3.  Approach the problem, not just the keyboard. Talk to users, understand the business. That is where the spec that AI cannot invent is born.
4.  Specialize in something that gives confidence. Security, critical systems, sensitive data: domains where human responsibility is non-negotiable.

And if you wonder if this means that AI is going to replace you, I analyze it thoroughly in [AI replacing programmers](/en/is-ai-replacing-software-developers): the summary is that it replaces tasks, not people with criteria.

## Frequently asked questions

If AI writes the code, why learn to program?
Because to direct, review and correct AI you need to understand what it produces. Those who do not know how to program cannot judge whether the code of a model is good, safe or correct. AI raises the ceiling of those who already have a base; it does not replace the base.

What skill should I prioritize in 2026 if I start?
The fundamentals (logic, data structures, how a system works) above any fashion framework. They are what gives you criteria, and the criterion is scarce. New framework you learn in a week; criterion, in years.

Are programmers' salaries going to fall?
It will lower the value of the automatizable tasks and will raise that of the scarce ones (design, decision, responsibility). The gap between those who only type and those who think is going to widen, not close.

Does this apply only to programming?
No. The same principle (value moves towards the scarce when something is automated) applies to design, marketing, law or medicine. Programming is only where you are seeing first and fastest.

## Conclusion: stop competing with the machine

The question "what is valuable now that everyone can program?" has an answer that has been true since Ada Lovelace, only now it is seen more clearly: what is valuable is human judgment that decides what to build and why.

AI is the best tool we have ever had to execute. Your job, more and more, is to be the one who decides what is worth executing. Stop competing with the machine in typing speed (that race you already lost, and nothing happens) and compete where she can not follow you: in criterion, in context and in responsibility.
